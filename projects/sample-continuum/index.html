Explore the illustrative evidence

The starting assumption was that a shorter referral form would improve completion. An illustrative workflow audit instead identified an ambiguous boundary: a local save looked like a successful transfer. Removing fields would leave that ambiguity intact.

Imagine a moderated task in which eight fictional participants prepare a referral and explain what happens next. Four reach the intended destination without help. Several describe the final message as proof that the clinic has received the record, even in an offline scenario. These are constructed observations, not interviews or tests that took place.

The useful distinction is between completing a screen and completing a handoff. The reframed opportunity is to make responsibility visible before submission and delivery observable afterward. That leads to a destination review, separate delivery states, and a receiving queue.

For a real project, retain the task script, anonymized observation notes, participant roles, and competing explanations. A missed action might also reflect training, terminology, or an unrealistic test scenario. Evidence should narrow the decision rather than conveniently prove an already chosen design.

08 / KEY PRODUCT DECISIONS

Three choices.
One clearer contract.

Guide / Product decisions

Keep the choice, reasoning, trade-off, and consequence visible. Put extended alternatives in the disclosure. Replace the illustrative collaboration with who actually influenced each decision, and distinguish what you owned from what the team decided.

01

Name the next owner.

The illustrative task suggests a status label alone is insufficient. Choose an explicit destination review over another generic alert. In the sample team, the programme lead clarifies who can receive the referral. The extra review step costs a tap but gives the worker a concrete handoff to confirm.

Consequence: destination and next action appear together before sending.

02

Saving is not sending.

Choose a local queue over an always-online submission gate. In this scenario, engineering identifies unreliable connectivity as the limiting condition. Preserving work adds retry and reconciliation complexity; it also prevents a connection failure from erasing progress.

Consequence: “Queued” keeps ownership with the sender until receipt.

03

Give each role a useful queue.

The illustrative workflow audit gives each role a different next action. Choose responsibility-based queues over one universal dashboard. The fictional product manager prioritizes actionability over a comprehensive landing screen. Some cross-role detail moves one level deeper, while a shared status vocabulary preserves continuity.

Consequence: workers see unsent handoffs; coordinators see referrals to accept.

Compare alternatives and trade-offs

Ownership: a passive confirmation banner is inexpensive but easy to dismiss. A blocking alert adds emphasis without explaining the receiving role. A destination review exposes the relationship before commitment. It is justified only if the consequence of a wrong handoff outweighs the extra step; routine repeat referrals might eventually support a remembered destination.

Connectivity: an always-online gate keeps the server authoritative but interrupts fieldwork. Silent background retries preserve progress yet obscure whether anyone received the record. An explicit queue gives the worker an honest state and a recovery action. A production version would need durable local storage, acknowledgement handling, an idempotency key, and a policy for stale records. The demonstration only simulates those states in memory.

Queues: a universal dashboard makes broad reporting easy but forces each role to filter irrelevant work. Separate products risk inconsistent statuses. Role-specific views of the same referral model offer a middle path. The trade-off is additional permission and navigation design, including a clear supervisor route to cross-role exceptions.

These choices form a connected argument. Naming an owner would be misleading without a trustworthy delivery state, and trustworthy states would be hard to act on without useful queues. A real case study should expose this dependency and include disagreement, rejected options, and the actual limits of your influence.

Explore recovery, permissions, and edge cases

The difficult cases sit between states: an interrupted acknowledgement, a retry after an uncertain response, a destination that becomes unavailable, or two people editing the same record. In a real implementation, the team would define retry limits, server reconciliation, audit history, and permission checks. A status label cannot substitute for those guarantees.

The prototype is deliberately narrower. It demonstrates a required destination, reversible review, an offline queue, and one simulated delivery. Restart clears its memory. Guide / Validation and iteration

Connect an observed problem to an actual change and a subsequent check. Identify the task and participant group. A prototype result is not a production outcome, and a small retest does not establish causation.

Read the illustrative validation notes

The comparison uses eight fictional participants and one defined task: select a destination, confirm the referral, and explain who acts next. Unassisted completion rises from four of eight to seven of eight in the invented retest. Median task time changes from 150 to 105 seconds. The arithmetic is a 30% reduction; the numbers are not empirical evidence.

The remaining failure matters. One fictional participant still interprets a queued handoff as received. The next iteration would strengthen the distinction through the confirmation heading and the sender’s remaining action, then retest in realistic connectivity conditions. Adding another success icon would contradict that finding.

A real study should document recruitment, facilitation, failed tasks, timing rules, and whether participants repeated the task. Familiarity, a small sample, or a changed testing environment could explain improvement. Production follow-up should examine delivery failures and completed referrals before claiming operational impact.

Explore the next validation bet

The strongest part of this concept is the contract between state and ownership. Preserve that relationship while testing whether the review step earns its cost. If workers reliably choose the same clinic, a remembered destination might reduce effort without hiding accountability.

The largest uncertainty is what happens after the device says “Sent.” A production pilot would need to trace acknowledged delivery, clinic acceptance, and follow-up separately. Improvements in prototype task time cannot establish improvements in access to care.

The next useful bet is a bounded field trial of the handoff language and exception queue, with engineering defining recovery behavior before release. Keep the outcome open: if participants still mistake queued work for delivered work, change the interaction rather than explaining the existing design more forcefully.
